## **Animal preparation**

All animal procedures were performed strictly following the guidelines of Tsinghua University and approved by its Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Mice were maintained in the Laboratory Animal Research Center at Tsinghua University, Beijing, under controlled conditions (temperature: 24°C; humidity: 50%; reversed light/dark cycle, lights on 7:00 AM–7:00 PM).

Experimental mice were generated by crossing Rasgrf2-2A-dCre mice (JAX 022864) with Ai148 (TIT2L-GC6f-ICL-tTA2)-D mice (JAX 030328), resulting in Cre-dependent expression of GCaMP6f (Jackson Laboratory). Food and water were provided ad libitum before surgery. Following surgery, mice were individually housed and habituated to head fixation for at least one week before recordings began.

## **Craniotomy operation for wide-field imaging**

Mice were anesthetized using isoflurane (4% induction, 1–2% maintenance, with 1 mL/min O₂) via an anesthesia machine (R520 IP, RWD Life Science Co.) and received meloxicam (5 mg/kg, subcutaneously) as analgesia. All surgical instruments and workstations were sterilized by autoclaving before use. Mice were securely positioned and leveled in a stereotaxic apparatus (68018, RWD Life Science Co.) with body temperature maintained at 37.0°C by a heating pad (RWD Life Science Co.).

For all-optical experiments, the scalp was removed, and a 7-mm-diameter craniotomy was created over the left monocular visual cortex (center coordinates: 1.3 mm lateral, 2.5 mm anterior to lambda). A chronic imaging window was implanted in place of the skull, secured with Krazy Glue (Elmer’s Products Inc.). An aluminum headplate featuring a 10-mm-diameter circular imaging well was attached over the cranial window using dental cement, allowing wide-field optical imaging of single neurons across the visual cortex.

After surgery, mice received daily subcutaneous injections of flunixin meglumine (1.25 mg/kg, Sichuan Dingjian Animal Medicine Co.) for five days to manage inflammation. Mice were allowed to recover for at least seven days, during which cranial window integrity and calcium imaging quality were assessed. Only fully recovered mice without distress signs were included in experiments. The procedure exhibited long-term stability, with operated mice showing normal behavior and survival beyond one year.

Neuronal calcium activity was recorded using the RUSH3D-Confocal system, a computational mesoscope based on scanning light-field microscopy (Zhang et al., 2024) with a confocal line-scanning strategy(Lu et al., 2024). The RUSH3D system is equipped with a custom macro objective (0.5 NA) and a large-format sCMOS camera (CMV50000, CMOSIS) (Zhang et al., 2024). To overcome the resolution limit of the microlens array (pitch 69 µm), a piezo tip/tilt actuator was employed to perform a 3 × 3 sub-microlens scanning strategy during acquisition. This approach oversamples the light field, providing centimeter-scale, high-speed volumetric sampling with a uniform resolution of ~2.6 × 2.6 × 6 µm³ resolution across a native field of view (FOV) of ~8 × 6 × 0.4 mm³. To achieve high-contrast volumetric imaging, the confocal scanning light-field microscopy (csLFM) integrates axially elongated line-confocal illumination with the rolling shutter in scanning light-field microscopy, effectively creating a "virtual slit" to physically reject out-of-focus background fluorescence with both optical sectioning and low phototoxicity (Lu et al., 2024).

Fluorescence was excited using a 488 nm LED source (LDI-488, 89 North) with low phototoxicity (~10% of the maximum output, ~0.12 mW/mm²). Volumetric data was acquired at a volume rate of 4 Hz.

Temporal alignment between imaging frames and visual stimuli was synchronized using the Medusa data acquisition system (Bio-Signal Technologies). The mesoscope RUSH3D-Confocal system generated trigger signals that were fed into this system, ensuring precise timing for the imaging of neural activity. To precisely register stimulus onset, a photosensitive resistor (LDR) monitored a dedicated small black square displayed in the lower-right corner of the stimulus monitor. Luminance transitions of this square produced sharp voltage changes, which were captured by the Medusa system in parallel with the imaging triggers. This optical-electrical cue allowed for frame-accurate post-hoc alignment of stimulus timing with the recorded volumetric data.

## **Fluorescence image processing and neuron extraction @桂沄**

## Pipeline (Old, based on ssid)

### Data Processing and Neuron Extraction Pipeline

To handle the large-scale 3D light-field data acquired by the RUSH3D system, we utilized the standard computational pipeline provided by the system developers **(Zhang et al., 2024)**. The processing workflow consists of the following key steps:

**Pixel Realignment and Preprocessing:** Raw sensor data were computationally realigned to synthesize high-resolution phase-space views. To facilitate efficient parallel computing and handle the centimeter-scale field of view (FOV), the data were partitioned into overlapping tiles.

**Multiscale Background Rejection (MBR):** To mitigate strong background fluorescence inherent in wide-field imaging of thick tissue, the Multiscale Background Rejection (MBR) algorithm was applied **(Zhang et al., 2021)**. Spatially nonuniform backgrounds were estimated from down-sampled phase-space views and subtracted from the raw measurements to enhance the signal-to-background ratio (SBR).

**Motion Correction and Seed Generation:** Following background rejection, rigid motion correction was performed on the phase-space data using the NoRMCorre algorithm **(Pnevmatikakis and Giovannucci, 2017)** to stabilize tissue movements. Initial neuron coordinates (seeds) were generated by computing a standard deviation projection of the motion-corrected video. This summary image was then reconstructed using Wave-optics Digital Adaptive Optics (wDAO) **(Wu et al., 2021)** to correct for system- and tissue-induced aberrations. Bright neurons within the aberration-corrected volume were identified via morphological segmentation to serve as initial spatial footprints $A_{\mathrm{init}}$.

**Sparse Seeded Iteration Demixing (SSID):** For high-speed signal extraction, the Sparse Seeded Iteration Demixing (SSID) algorithm was employed **(Zhang et al., 2024)**. Instead of reconstructing every frame, SSID extracts neuronal signals directly in the light-field space using only a sparse subset of angular views (e.g., 13 out of 225 views) to reduce computational redundancy. The algorithm utilizes a constrained non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) approach, iteratively updating spatial footprints $A$ and temporal traces $F$ by minimizing the residual between the measured sparse light-field data and the projection of estimated neuronal components.

## Pipeline(New, DL enhanced)

### **High-Throughput Data Processing Pipeline**

To address the computational challenges imposed by the massive data throughput of the RUSH3D system, we established a high-performance, parallelized computational pipeline. This pipeline features a distributed architecture leveraging multi-GPU acceleration and TensorRT optimization to achieve millisecond-scale inference speeds. The processing workflow is divided into three distinct stages:

1. Preprocessing and Rigid Motion Correction

The raw scanning light-field data were computationally partitioned into a $5 \times 7$ grid of overlapping patches (blocks) to facilitate distributed parallel processing. For each patch, geometric optical distortion was corrected block-wise based on pre-calibrated parameters, followed by pixel realignment to synthesize high-resolution phase-space views.

To mitigate artifacts arising from tissue pulsation and respiration, we implemented a rigid motion correction module based on the **NoRMCorre** algorithm **(Pnevmatikakis and Giovannucci, 2017)**. Given that the relative optical alignment between angular views in light-field microscopy remains constant, we adopted an efficient correction strategy: rigid motion vectors were estimated solely from the central angular view (View 0) of the phase-space. These vectors were then globally applied to all simultaneous angular views within the same time point. This approach preserves phase-space consistency while significantly reducing computational overhead.

2. Deep-Learning-Based Real-time 3D Reconstruction

We employed a deep-learning framework, termed SERENet (Spatially Embedded Reconstruction Network), to rapidly reconstruct high-resolution 3D volumetric stacks from light-field data. The reconstruction module, accelerated via TensorRT, consists of two key components:

- **Implicit Aberration Correction (DL-PreDAO):** Prior to volumetric reconstruction, a grid-sampling-based deep learning module (DL-PreDAO) was utilized to implicitly model and correct complex optical aberrations induced by both the system and the biological specimen. This module predicts a deformation grid to spatially transform the input phase-space views, optimizing ray alignment before reconstruction.
- **Volumetric Reconstruction:** The aberration-corrected data were first projected into standard deviation (STD) maps to enhance the contrast of neuronal structures. The SERENet core network then directly mapped these STD projections to high-resolution 3D neuronal volumes.
- **Network Training:** The network was trained using a supervised learning approach with paired experimental data. We acquired ground-truth 3D volumes using two-photon microscopy and simultaneously captured light-field images of the same field of view for network optimization.

3. 3D Segmentation and Signal Extraction

Neuronal identification within the reconstructed volumes was performed using a 3D U-Net segmentation network. The network assigned a probability score to each voxel, semantically segmenting connected neuronal regions of interest (ROIs) from the background.

For high-throughput temporal signal extraction, the generated 3D segmentation masks were projected back onto the motion-corrected time-series volumes. We extracted the raw fluorescence trace $F_{\mathrm{raw}}(t)$ for each neuron and the surrounding neuropil trace $F_{\mathrm{neuropil}}(t)$. To correct for local background contamination, we applied the standard neuropil subtraction formula (Chen et al., 2013):

$$
F_{\mathrm{corr}}(t) = F_{\mathrm{raw}}(t) - \alpha F_{\mathrm{neuropil}}(t)
$$

where the subtraction coefficient $\alpha$ was set to 0.24. Finally, extracted neurons from all 35 patches were stitched based on their spatial coordinates to generate a seamless, cortex-wide functional map.

## **Intrinsic signal imaging @朱彦霖**

To functionally delineate the boundaries of visual cortical areas and establish a reference map for subsequent data registration, we performed intrinsic signal optical imaging. This method leverages the coupling between neuronal activity and hemodynamics to visualize the retinotopic organization of the mouse visual cortex across a large field of view, enabling the precise identification of the primary visual cortex (V1) and surrounding higher-order areas prior to calcium imaging.

Visual stimuli were generated using a custom Python script based on PsychoPy and displayed on a 60-Hz LCD monitor (1920 × 1080 resolution; 60 cm width, 35 cm height). The monitor was positioned 15 cm from the mouse’s left eye, angled at 30° relative to the anteroposterior axis to cover the binocular and monocular visual fields. To correct for the geometric distortion caused by the flat screen at a close viewing distance, a spherical correction algorithm was applied to the stimuli (Zhuang et al., 2017). The visual stimuli consisted of a drifting checkerboard bar (25° width) moving in four cardinal directions (up, down, left, right) to map altitude and azimuth coordinates. The bar drifted across the full screen with a period of [e.g., 10 or 20] s, repeated for [number] trials for each direction.

During the imaging session, mice were lightly anesthetized with isoflurane ([0.5–1.0]%) to maintain physiological stability while preserving hemodynamic responses. Optical imaging was performed using a custom wide-field microscopy system equipped with a high-sensitivity sCMOS camera (PCO.Panda 4.2, PCO AG). The brain surface was illuminated with a [e.g., 630 nm] red LED for functional imaging of intrinsic hemodynamic signals and a [e.g., 525 nm] green LED for structural imaging of the surface vasculature. Images were acquired at a frame rate of 10 Hz with a spatial resolution of 1312 × 1304 pixels (after 2× spatial binning). Image acquisition was synchronized with the visual stimulus presentation using a hardware trigger (NI-DAQ, National Instruments).

Retinotopic maps were computed using the Fourier analysis method (Kalatsky and Stryker, 2003). For each pixel, the phase of the signal at the stimulus frequency was extracted to determine the retinotopic location. To cancel the hemodynamic delay, phase maps obtained from stimuli drifting in opposite directions were subtracted. Altitude and azimuth maps were then combined to calculate the visual field sign map (sin(gradient_azimuth − gradient_altitude)). Visual areas, including the primary visual cortex (V1) and higher-order visual areas (LM, AL, RL, AM, PM), were segmented based on the sign reversals in the visual field sign map (Garrett et al., 2014; Zhuang et al., 2017).

Finally, these functional maps were registered to the Allen Mouse Brain Common Coordinate Framework (CCFv3) using a custom MATLAB pipeline. First, a structural image of the surface vasculature was acquired under green light illumination. Landmarks (e.g., vessel intersections) were manually identified on both the experimental vessel image and the CCF vessel template. An initial affine transformation (scaling, rotation, and translation) was calculated to align the experimental image to the CCF space. The registration was further refined by aligning the functional boundaries of V1 derived from the retinotopic mapping with the anatomical borders in the CCF atlas. This registration matrix was subsequently applied to the calcium imaging data to assign recorded neurons to specific visual areas.
